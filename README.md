## Essential Questions

### How do I interpret user needs and implement them into a program? How does creating "user stories" help with this?

Interpreting user needs begins with active listening and collaboration with stakeholders, then translating what they want into specific, actionable requirements. Rather than gathering a static list of features upfront, I've learned to treat user needs as evolving — something that deepens as both the developer and the client gain a clearer picture of the product over time.

Creating user stories is one of the most effective tools for bridging the gap between client expectations and technical implementation. A well-written user story — formatted as *"As a [user], I want to [goal] so that [reason]"* — keeps the focus on value and intent rather than technical jargon. During the SNHU Travel project, for example, the story *"As a traveler, I want to filter destinations by budget so that I can find affordable options"* immediately communicated the user's perspective and gave the development team a clear acceptance target. It also made estimation and task decomposition straightforward, breaking the story down into database, UI, and integration tasks.

User stories also create a shared vocabulary between the Product Owner, developers, and stakeholders. When the client requested an interactive travel map mid-project, the Product Owner was able to quickly write it as a user story, add it to the backlog, and prioritize it — without disrupting the current sprint. This ability to capture new needs in a structured, comparable format is what makes user stories so powerful for managing a product that evolves alongside user feedback.

### How do I approach developing programs? What Agile processes do I hope to incorporate into my future development work?

My approach to developing programs is iterative and incremental. Rather than trying to design everything perfectly upfront, I prefer to deliver small, working pieces of functionality early and often, then refine them based on feedback. This mindset aligns closely with Agile values, and it has proven effective throughout this course.

Working on the SNHU Travel application reinforced several Agile practices I intend to carry into my future work. Sprint planning helped me think carefully about scope — committing only to what could realistically be completed in a fixed timeframe while keeping long-term goals visible in the backlog. Daily standups taught me the value of consistent, brief communication: surfacing blockers early enough to address them before they become sprint-killers. The Definition of Done gave me a clear quality standard that prevented the habit of calling something "finished" before it was truly ready.

Looking ahead, I want to make retrospectives a permanent part of how I work, even on solo projects. Reflecting on what went well and what could be improved after every major milestone — not just at the end — creates a feedback loop that compounds over time. I also plan to use burndown charts or similar visual tracking tools to maintain honest visibility into progress, rather than relying on gut instinct. Above all, I hope to carry forward the Agile principle of welcoming change rather than resisting it. Requirements will always shift, and building a process that accommodates that reality produces better outcomes than pretending they won't.

### What does it mean to be a good team member in software development?

Being a good team member in software development means more than writing clean code. It means communicating proactively, owning your work collectively, and supporting the people around you as much as your assigned tasks.

Throughout the SNHU Travel project, I observed how each role contributed to the team's success in distinct ways — and how easily things could break down when communication faltered. When the Scrum Master intervened to resolve an API miscommunication between front-end and back-end developers, it wasn't just a technical fix; it was an act of team stewardship that kept the sprint on track. That kind of active support — raising issues early, resolving blockers quickly, and keeping the team aligned — is what separates good teams from great ones.

Being a good team member also means being adaptable. When a developer became unavailable mid-sprint due to illness, the rest of the team absorbed the work without blame or resentment. That flexibility is only possible when team members invest in shared understanding of the codebase and collective ownership of the backlog. It also requires a culture of psychological safety — one where raising concerns is welcomed, not punished. The daily standup's consistent format ("What did I do? What will I do? What's blocking me?") modeled this well: it normalized transparency and made asking for help a routine part of the process, not an admission of failure.

Ultimately, being a good team member means showing up with both technical competence and genuine care for the team's success — treating the product as something you all own together, not just a list of tickets assigned to individuals.
